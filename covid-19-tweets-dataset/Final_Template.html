Introduction¶

In a world that has become increasingly digitized, social media platforms such as Twitter have become an important tool that not only reflects the public’s sentiments but actively shapes them. During major global events such as the recent COVID-19 pandemic, these platforms become spaces where information, misinformation, and public discourse occur. Understanding how public sentiment on social media platforms responds to and influences pandemic outcomes such as death and vaccination rates offers important insights into public opinion and the potential of digital platforms to affect real-world outcomes. This paper explores the connection between social media sentiment and the COVID-19 pandemic through two major research questions which both aim to understand the dynamic between social media behaviour and pandemic-related data. The questions are as follows:

  • RQ1: Can we classify public sentiment toward COVID-19 vaccines on Twitter to be positive, neutral, or negative based on death trends, vaccination rates, and user-level information from December 2020 to March 2022?
  • RQ2: The second question investigates if daily COVID-19 death counts could be predicted using vaccination coverage, seasonal context, vaccine-type information, public sentiment on social media, and daily vaccination data from December 2020 to March 2022?

There have been similar prior studies that have explored classifying tweet sentiments related to COVID-19 using deep learning models, such as the hybrid TCNN-BiLSTM model (Aslan 2022), which achieved high accuracy in sentiment classification tasks. However, while this research helps in our understanding of how to label and analyze text sentiment, it does not examine how text sentiment correlates with real-world metrics like vaccination or death rates. Our project builds on this foundation by not only analyzing tweet sentiment but also exploring its potential impact alongside public health data. In doing so, we expand the topic beyond classification to explore causality, influence, and impact between digital discourse and real-world outcomes. Understanding these connections between social media sentiment and real-world outcomes could inform public health officials, policymakers, and digital strategists on how to better respond to health crises, whether by identifying misinformation early or tailoring vaccination campaigns to digital trends. These findings may also relate to other fields that are not health-related, such as political campaigning and marketing.

Data¶

To answer our research questions, we combined data from three distinct sources, each offering a unique perspective on the global COVID-19 pandemic. The datasets included daily death tolls, vaccine-related tweets, and vaccination rates across countries. These were carefully cleaned, filtered, and merged by the variable Date to build a final dataset with 2,000 observations and 16 relevant variables.

The first dataset contains the daily COVID-19 death toll worldwide and was scraped using BeautifulSoup from Worldometer, a well-known statistics aggregation site. While hosted on Worldometer, the data itself was compiled from official government sources such as the National Health Commission (NHC) of the People’s Republic of China and the Health Commission of Hubei Province. It spans over three years, from January 24, 2020, to April 12, 2024, providing 1,549 observations. From this dataset, we used the following variables:

  • Date: the day the deaths were recorded (datetime),
  • daily_increase_percent: the percentage change in deaths from the previous day (float), and
  • Season: a derived categorical variable representing the season (Winter, Spring, Summer, Fall) based on the date.

The second dataset consists of over 100,000 tweets related to the COVID-19 vaccine. It was obtained from Kaggle, but the original source of data is a collection of tweets containing the hashtag #CovidVaccine, captured between January 8, 2020, and an unspecified end date. The relevant variables we used from this dataset are:

  • Date: the UTC timestamp when the tweet was posted (datetime64),
  • text_sentiment: sentiment score of the tweet as positive, neutral, or negative, derived using the SentimentIntensityAnalyzer() from NLTK, (category)
  • user_verified: whether the account is verified (boolean),
  • user_since_days: number of days since the user's account was created (int), calculated by subtracting user_created from Date, and
  • user_location: the geographical location listed in the user’s profile (cleaned and renamed to country for merging) (category).

The third dataset, also from Kaggle, includes daily country-level COVID-19 vaccination rates. The original data was collected from the Our World in Data GitHub repository, spanning from December 1, 2020, to March 28, 2022. From this dataset, we used: Date: the day the vaccination data was recorded (datetime64),

  • total_vaccinations_per_hundred: number of vaccine doses administered per 100 people (float64),
  • vaccine_group: a cleaned categorical variable classifying vaccines into groups such as mRNA, Viral Vector, Inactivated, etc.
  • country: the country name associated with the vaccination data, used for merging (category).
  • daily_vaccinations: the number of vaccines administered per day (float64).

After merging the datasets on the Date column and standardizing the country field, we had about 15,000 observations. To ensure balanced representation and reduce the dataset size for analysis, we sampled 100 tweets from each recorded month, bringing the final dataset to 2,000 rows and 16 variables. Extensive cleaning was performed. Date formats across all datasets were standardized to %Y-%m-%d. For the tweet dataset, user_created was also converted to datetime. In the death toll dataset, column names with excess whitespace were cleaned. For user_location, we removed special characters, extra spaces, and irrelevant entries like "moon," "earth," and "nowhere." We then used a predefined mapping and the pycountry library to identify valid countries; rows with unmatched or null values were dropped. Missing values were also addressed. For country mismatches in tweet data, if no valid location was found, the entry was removed. After combining all datasets, any rows with missing values in key variables were dropped, as imputing or estimating them would risk introducing bias into our sentiment and vaccine trend analyses. Additional derived variables were created for analysis:

  • Season (category) from the Date (datetime64) column,
  • user_since_days (int64) from user_created(datetime64),
  • text_sentiment (category) using sentiment scoring (SentimentIntensityAnalyzer()), and
  • vaccine_group, (category) which grouped nearly 80 vaccine types into 4 main categories using a custom mapping function.

Overall, our final dataset brings together key information on global COVID-19 deaths, vaccination rates, and public sentiment from tweets, covering the period between December 25, 2020, and March 29, 2022. Each row reflects data for a specific country and date, combining tweet sentiment with vaccination and mortality stats. Results¶

After doing our initial exploratory data analysis, a lingering question that we needed to answer was: can we predict the tweet sentiment in which a tweet was posted using variables such as daily_death increase, daily_vaccinations, user_verification status, and the user_since_days i.e. age of the account? Tweet sentiment was classified into three categories: negative (-1), neutral (0), and positive (1). To answer this question, we fitted two models to the data. The first model is a baseline model that predicts each sentiment group equally. This dummy model achieved an accuracy of 30%, a macro-averaged F1-score of 0.29, and a weighted F1-score of 0.32. These numbers are consistent with expectations for random prediction across three classes, where pure chance would correctly guess approximately one-third of the time. The confusion matrix shows that the model’s predictions are spread randomly across all classes, and the classification report confirms that precision, recall, and F1-scores are low across the board. This baseline does not capture any meaningful relationship between the input features and the sentiment labels, and it serves only as a reference point.

In order to find the best model that outperforms our baseline, we attempted to fit a K-Nearest Neighbors model (K-NN), a Support Vector Machine (SVM), and a Random Forest Classifier. From this, we determined that the Random Forest Classifier was the best due to its higher accuracy, macro-averaged F1 score, and weighted F1 score. These numbers for the K-NN model were 0.31, 0.30, and 0.32, respectively. For the support vector machine, we found our accuracy and F1 scores to be 0.46, 0.21, and 0.15. For our Random Forest Classifier, it was 0.37, 0.35, and 0.37. The accuracy for the SVM is higher than that of the Random Forest, however, the F1 scores are considerably lower. Because the numbers for the Random Forest Classifier are the most consistent across all three groups, this was the model we chose. There is a bar chart below that compares the three model choices.

Going deeper into the confusion matrix for the Random Forest Classifier, we see that the precision for each sentiment class of negative, neutral, and positive is 0.28, 0.28, and 0.48, respectively. This means that 28% of tweets predicted as negative and neutral were accurate, while 48% of tweets predicted as positive were accurate. For the recall, 27% of tweets predicted as negative were correctly identified, and 30% of tweets predicted as neutral were correctly identified. 47% of positive tweets were correctly identified. The F1 score, which balances precision and recall, is best for the positive class at 0.47, and for the negative and neutral classes it is 0.28 and 0.29, respectively. The macro-average F1 treats all classes equally, and it is the same across both precision and recall, with a value of 0.35. The weighted average is determined by how many examples of each class there are, and this value is 0.37. Overall, an accuracy of 0.37 is not ideal, but it is still better than our baseline model.

Now that we have the information from the confusion matrix, we should check each predictor individually to see how important each one is for predicting tweet sentiment. To do this, we created a visualization that measures the feature importance of each predictor. In the case of Random Forests, it measures how much each feature helped reduce impurity across all the decision trees. The chart shows that the most important features of predicting tweet sentiment are user_since_days and daily_vaccinations. The number of days since an account was created is the most significant feature, and daily vaccinations are the second most important feature. A daily increase in vaccinations and the verification status of a Twitter user are shown to be of less importance when predicting tweet sentiment. This plot shows that sentiment is influenced by public health trends as well as user-level characteristics.

Overall, the Random Forest Classifier achieved an overall accuracy of 37%, outperforming the random guessing baseline of 30%. It shows that the model is able to extract some meaningful patterns from the available numeric features, though its predictive power remains limited. The classification report highlights that the model performs best on positive sentiment tweets (class 1), while it struggles more with negative (-1) and neutral (0) classes. The macro-averaged F1-score of 0.35 and weighted F1-score of 0.37 indicate moderate but consistent performance across the classes. The confusion matrix confirms that the model most accurately identifies positive tweets but often confuses negative and neutral sentiments with positive ones. These results suggest that while Random Forest captures some relationship between the input features and tweet sentiment, the features currently used (user_since_days, daily_vaccinations, daily_increase_percent, user_verified) provide a limited signal. Nevertheless, the Random Forest model demonstrates real learning beyond random guessing and serves as a stronger baseline for comparison against more complex models.

For our second question, we created many models to try and most accurately predict daily_deaths using total_vaccinations_per_hundred, Season, country, vaccine_group, text_sentiment, and daily_vaccinations. To start, vaccine_group was deemed an unnecessary feature because of its high VIF score of thirty, so it was removed from all modeling processes. After this, the training and testing sets were created, with a baseline model being fit based on the mean of the training set. This model had an R-squared value of -0.0003 and an RMSE of 2877.8. Thus, just predicting daily_deaths based on the mean would net us an approximate average error of about 2878, which is far off of what we were looking for. After this baseline model was fit, multiple regression models, such as Linear, Elastic Net, K-Nearest Neighbors, Support Vector, and Random Forest, were fit to compare their performance to the baseline model. The hyperparameters for each of these models were chosen by doing grid searches for each model and continuously improving them until nothing else could be done. This allowed us to create models with minimal bias and variance of each modeling strategy, letting us decide which is truly the most effective. For the best model with each technique, the R-squared value and RMSE were calculated, and the model-by-model comparison of these values is shown below.

As seen by this barplot, the Random Forest Model is outperforming all other models, which is verified by the minimized RMSE value of 809.6 and maximized R-squared value of 0.9208 on the testing set. To tune the model to be this accurate, several grid searches and cross-validations were done to find the best tree depth, which ended up being twelve, offering a good balance between bias and variance. A depth this deep also accounted for possible interaction terms or polynomial terms, without overanalyzing too much. Based on this finalized model, the importance of features for predicting daily_deaths was, in order of the greatest to the least importance, total_vaccinations_per_hundred, daily_vaccinations, Season, country, and lastly text_sentiment. It is important to note here that total_vaccinations_per_hundred can account for up to 60% of the variation in daily_deaths, which is logical and follows a trend for many viral studies. These predictors were doing a very good job because although the RMSE is about 810, which may seem large, it was a very minimal average error in the context of deaths from COVID-19. Although our model proved fairly accurate with a reasonably low RMSE and high R-squared value, it still faced many limitations. Since we were only able to train on about 1,500 data points, the reliability of the model is certainly reduced in comparison to if we had something like 15,000 points. Along with that, there may have been other incidents worldwide leading to random spikes in deaths from COVID-19, such as natural disasters or people being forced into close contact. This could lead to spikes in daily deaths for some countries or regions, possibly affecting the accuracy when predicting. For future cases, this could be expanded on with more data and potentially be used to predict an outbreak of another disease and the dangers it may pose for populations by country and the world, based on the features used for predicting daily_deaths in our case with COVID-19.

Overall, our modeling strategy for predicting daily_deaths was very accurate, with the Random Forest capturing polynomial and interaction trends better than any other models. Our features accounted for about 92.1% of the variance within our testing data, proving its worth, and along with that, the average error was minimized to generally place our predicted values within about 10% of the actual testing values, which is reasonably strong. Following the results from this question, we can ascertain that one of the most important things in preventing deaths from disease, and especially COVID-19 in our case, is simply getting a vaccine. With vaccines being the most important predictor and accounting for a majority of the variance, it is essential to note that an increase in vaccination rates leads to a direct decrease in our predicted daily_deaths. Along that note, an interesting topic to investigate would be how social media sentiment affects vaccination counts and rates, which in today’s world, is an especially polarising and debatable topic.

Conclusion¶

Our original questions asked whether tweet sentiment could be predicted using death increases, vaccinations, user verification, and account age, and whether daily deaths could be predicted from vaccination rates, season, country, vaccine type, sentiment, and vaccinations. We found that newer accounts and verified users leaned more positive, while older accounts leaned neutral or negative; higher daily vaccinations correlated with more positive or neutral tweets. Higher vaccination rates generally correlated with lower daily deaths, influenced by season and vaccine type. A Random Forest model was the most effective for predicting daily deaths, with cross-validation and R² scores helping to optimize model complexity. Overall, our findings suggest user credibility and vaccination efforts are stronger predictors of sentiment than pandemic severity, supporting prior research linking public sentiment to health interventions.

Despite the rare pandemic occurrence, the results highlight the importance of understanding how people may respond to a global health crisis. As shown in our findings, public sentiment was more closely tied to individual user characteristics, such as account age, rather than direct public health indicators. Meanwhile, vaccination data proved to be a far more reliable predictor of daily COVID-19 deaths. This reveals a key distinction of emotional reactions on social media that may not always align with real-world health outcomes, whereas widespread vaccination efforts have a demonstrable impact in mitigating the effects of a pandemic. Beyond the context of the pandemic, this research can be used to better understand how people react to dire and urgent events. With concerns about propaganda or false information spreading, it is crucial to study how information circulates during life-threatening situations—such as natural disasters like hurricanes—and how the public’s responses could correlate with or impact lives at risk. Outside of the pandemic and natural disasters, the results of the Tweet sentiments during COVID-19 display how social media platforms could improve user behavior and potentially develop tools to identify and mitigate the spread of harmful content or promote constructive conversations. Social media platforms could apply insights like these to promote more positive behavior and prevent the spread of misinformation.

However, there were some limitations to our data exploration, such as the random selection of users, which may have led to a sample that is not fully representative of the population. Another potential issue was the measurement of tweet sentiment. For instance, the article “Sentiment Analysis of COVID-19 Tweets Using Deep Learning Models” explores tweet sentiments during the pandemic lockdown in greater depth, using classifications such as “joy,” “sadness,” “anger,” and “fear.” In contrast, the dataset used in our project categorizes sentiment only as negative, positive, or neutral. To gain a better understanding of public sentiment during the pandemic, a possible extension of this research could involve a more detailed analysis of emotional expression, such as identifying specific emotions conveyed in tweets. Another potential research direction is analyzing how world leaders' responses during the pandemic influenced national outcomes. Studying the sentiments expressed by presidents or prime ministers, alongside country-level health metrics, could reveal whether leadership styles contributed to better or worse pandemic results. Analyzing the sentiments expressed by these leaders, along with how their countries fared during the pandemic, could reveal whether their leadership helped or hindered national outcomes.
